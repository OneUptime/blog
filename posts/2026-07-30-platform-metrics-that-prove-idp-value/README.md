# Which Platform Engineering Metrics Actually Prove an Internal Developer Platform Is Working?

Author: [nawazdhandala](https://www.github.com/nawazdhandala)

Tags: Platform Engineering, Internal Developer Platform, Developer Experience, DORA, Metrics

Description: Build an evidence-based platform scorecard that connects adoption and reliability to less developer friction and better software delivery.

---

An internal developer platform is working when it makes valuable engineering work easier without hiding unacceptable reliability, security, or cost tradeoffs. A count of portal visits, templates, or platform features cannot prove that.

The useful evidence is a chain:

```text
platform capability
    -> developers choose and successfully use it
    -> a workflow becomes easier or faster
    -> delivery or operational outcomes improve
    -> guardrails remain healthy
```

No single metric covers that chain. A defensible scorecard combines measures from each stage and keeps the platform's contribution separate from claims of causation.

## Start With the Outcome, Not the Dashboard

Write a measurable product hypothesis for each platform investment:

> If teams provision standard environments through the platform, then the median request-to-ready time will fall from two days to under 30 minutes, while failed requests and policy exceptions remain below agreed limits.

That statement identifies:

- the customer and workflow;
- the behavior expected to change;
- the developer outcome;
- the guardrails; and
- the baseline against which to judge the change.

Without this step, teams tend to collect whatever their tools expose. The result is an attractive activity dashboard that cannot answer whether developers are better off.

## The Five-Layer Platform Scorecard

### 1. Reach and Voluntary Adoption

Measure whether the intended customers can and choose to use the capability:

```text
eligible-team adoption =
  teams using the capability in the window
  / teams for whom the capability is relevant
```

Also track active developers, active teams, eligible services covered, repeat use, and adoption by cohort. Exclude mandated migrations from a "voluntary adoption" series or label them separately. A login generated by single sign-on is not adoption, and one scaffold event is not sustained use.

Adoption is evidence of relevance, not proof of value. It becomes stronger when paired with a low abandonment rate and repeat use across real production workflows.

### 2. Task Success and Self-Service

For a named task, measure whether a developer can reach the intended result without another team performing work:

```text
self-service rate =
  requests completed through the supported automated path
  / all eligible completed requests
```

Pair that rate with:

- successful completion rate;
- median and p90 completion time;
- abandonment or timeout rate;
- manual approval and handoff rate;
- support contacts per 100 attempts; and
- rollback or remediation rate.

A platform can report 90% "usage" while every request still waits two days for a manual approval. Task-level telemetry exposes that gap.

### 3. Developer Experience

Measure the experience directly rather than inferring it from clicks. Useful signals include:

- customer effort after a specific workflow;
- satisfaction with the platform capability;
- perceived ability to find the right path;
- interruptions and context switches;
- wait time in feedback loops; and
- reported cognitive load.

The SPACE framework warns against reducing developer productivity to one activity measure. Combine survey evidence with workflow telemetry. A faster build is useful evidence; developers reporting that builds are unpredictable explains why the measured gain may not translate into flow.

### 4. Delivery and Operational Outcomes

DORA's current software delivery performance model measures change lead time, deployment frequency, failed deployment recovery time, change fail rate, and deployment rework rate. These are application or service outcomes, not platform-team output metrics.

Use them to test whether a platform change is associated with better delivery for adopting services. Compare each service with its own baseline, and where possible use phased rollout cohorts:

```text
estimated platform-associated change =
  (after - before for adopters)
  - (after - before for comparable non-adopters)
```

Treat this as evidence, not automatic proof. Product complexity, staffing, release policy, and concurrent CI improvements can all affect the result. Preserve service-level context rather than averaging unrelated systems into one company number.

Platform-specific operational outcomes may include reduced setup lead time, fewer access-related incidents, faster vulnerability remediation, or fewer configuration-drift events.

### 5. Platform Reliability and Guardrails

A fast path that is frequently unavailable is not a successful product. Track:

- availability and latency against published SLOs;
- error-budget consumption;
- workflow failure and retry rates;
- time to restore the platform;
- security and policy exception rates;
- cost per completed workflow or supported service; and
- aged manual fallbacks.

These metrics create useful tension. Higher adoption alongside worse p90 latency, more exceptions, or rising support load is a scaling warning, not unqualified success.

## A Practical Scorecard

Keep the executive view small while allowing drill-down:

| Question | Primary metric | Guardrail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Are intended teams choosing it? | Voluntary eligible-team adoption | Bypass rate |
| Can they finish without help? | Self-service completion rate | Support contacts per 100 attempts |
| Is the workflow better? | Median and p90 end-to-end time | Failure and abandonment rate |
| Do developers feel the difference? | Transactional effort score | Response rate and cohort mix |
| Did delivery improve? | Service-level DORA trend | Reliability and rework |
| Can the platform sustain demand? | SLO attainment | Error-budget burn and unit cost |

Add diagnostic measures only when they help explain movement in these primary metrics. For example, break request-to-ready time into queue, approval, provisioning, and verification stages.

## Instrument the Whole Journey

Give every platform request a durable request ID and record:

```text
request_id, capability, team_id, service_id, environment
started_at, completed_at, outcome, failure_reason
interaction_mode, manual_handoffs, approval_wait_ms
platform_version, policy_exceptions
```

Join platform events to authoritative inventories and deployment records using stable team and service identifiers. Avoid email addresses, repository URLs, or mutable display names as join keys.

Define the denominator before launching a metric. "Services on the platform" is meaningless until "service," "eligible," "active," and the observation window are specified. Version every definition and retain raw events so a changed rule can be backfilled.

Use distributions rather than only averages. The median shows the typical path; p90 or p95 reveals whether a minority of teams still encounter severe friction. Segment by workflow, team type, environment, and adopter cohort, but suppress tiny groups to protect privacy.

## Run a Review That Leads to Decisions

Review platform metrics as a product team:

1. Choose one customer problem and record its baseline.
2. Set a target and balancing guardrails.
3. Ship the smallest platform change capable of affecting it.
4. Compare exposed and unexposed cohorts over the same period.
5. Interview outliers and teams that bypassed the platform.
6. Decide whether to expand, change, or retire the capability.

The decision log is as important as the chart. It prevents the organization from retroactively declaring whichever number moved to be the original goal.

## What Counts as Proof?

For an internal product, proof is cumulative. The strongest case looks like this:

- eligible teams adopt the capability without coercion;
- they complete the target task reliably and with fewer handoffs;
- measured task time and reported effort improve;
- adopting services show a sustained improvement in relevant delivery outcomes;
- reliability, security, and cost guardrails remain acceptable; and
- interviews and bypass analysis support the same explanation.

That is much harder to game than feature counts, and much more useful. The aim is not to prove that the platform team is busy. It is to show that the platform changed how engineering work happens for the better.
